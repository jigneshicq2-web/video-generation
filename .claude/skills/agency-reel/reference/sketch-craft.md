# Sketch craft for Client vs Agency reels

A working guide distilled from UCB, Second City, iO, sketch-writing books and famous sketches, adapted to our format: 20 to 80 s, 9:16, two stick figures. Rex (loud, sure of himself, always the foil) and Kate (calm agency owner, always wins). Audience: social-media agency owners. RecurPost appears only on the end card.

Source key: **UCB** = Upright Citizens Brigade Comedy Improvisation Manual (Besser, Roberts, Walsh, 2013); **UCB201** = UCB Sketch 201 class notes (drewtarvin.com/comedy/ucb-sketch-201-class-notes); **Chuffah** = "8 common sketch problems and how to fix them" (chuffah.substack.com); **Hines** = Will Hines on heighten and explore (funnyhow.substack.com/p/heighten-and-explore); **NYIT** = New York Improv Theater, "Sketch comedy 101" (newyorkimprovtheater.com, 2026); **Vorhaus** = John Vorhaus, *The Comic Toolbox*; **Close** = Halpern, Close, Johnson, *Truth in Comedy* (iO); **Johnstone** = Keith Johnstone, *Impro*.

## (a) Core principles

**1. One game per sketch.** The game is the single unusual thing plus the pattern of behaviour that repeats and grows. If you can't say it in one sentence, you don't have it yet (UCB; UCB201: "unusual thing + justification/specificity"). NYIT: "Do not introduce five different weird things."
*Example:* "Rex treats every metric as proof he is a genius, however bad it is."

**2. Base reality first, and fast.** Viewers must recognise the normal world before the unusual thing can register; miss that link at the start and they stay confused throughout (UCB201; Chuffah #5).
*Example:* Frame one: a monthly report meeting, a chart on the wall, name tags. Everyone already knows this room.

**3. The first unusual thing is the engine.** Find the first break in base reality, frame it (someone notices), then play it as a pattern (UCB).
*Example:* The chart is clearly falling. Rex: "Look at that. Downward trend. We're getting *deeper* into the market."

**4. "If this is true, what else is true?"** You heighten by following the unusual logic, not by inventing new weirdness. Every beat is a consequence of the first premise (UCB "If, Then"; UCB201).
*Example:* If Rex reads every drop as success, he also wants a bonus for the unfollows and asks Kate to "lose more followers, faster".

**5. Heighten and explore, alternately.** Heightening makes the behaviour bigger; exploring gives the reason behind it, which makes the next heightening believable. Hines: "do something, say why you're doing it, do something again." Repeating the same size of beat is boring (Chuffah #2, #4).
*Example:* Heighten: "Zero comments. Nobody could argue." Explore: "My dad said silence is respect." Heighten again.

**6. Play it straight; the character believes it.** The more ridiculous the situation, the more seriously it must be played (Close). Rex never winks; he is proud.
*Example:* Rex presents his 3 AM posting plan as a strategy, not a joke.

**7. Status is the see-saw.** Most comedy lowers someone's status (Johnstone). Kicking a high-status figure feels like a satisfying inversion; kicking a low-status one is bullying (Chuffah #7). Rex, the paying client, starts high, and the game lowers him. Kate stays level; she never rises by shouting, she rises because he falls.
*Example:* Rex enters with "I've been in business 20 years." He leaves quoting his mum's like count.

**8. The straight man must earn her place.** A straight man who only says "that's weird" slows the sketch; give her a motive and play her "to the top of her intelligence" (Chuffah #6; UCB201). Kate is not a victim like Anderson in "The Expert": she asks the precise question that makes Rex heighten.
*Example:* Kate: "What's the budget?" Nothing more. Rex's answer is the joke.

**9. Specific beats general.** Specific names, numbers and objects are funnier and clearer (UCB201: move general to specific). "Balakay" beats "a mispronounced name" (Key & Peele).
*Example:* Not "a bad logo" but "a photo of the sign, taken from his car, with the wiper in it".

**10. Truth plus pain.** Comedy is a real truth exaggerated until it hurts (Vorhaus). It must be something every agency owner has lived, which produces the "this is my client" comment ("The Expert": "This is our company").
*Example:* The client who wants "Nike quality" on a corner-shop budget.

**11. Don't explain the joke; trust the viewer.** Close: "Treat your audience like poets and geniuses." But clarity isn't cleverness: the audience will not work a joke out afterwards (UCB201: "Don't try to be clever"). Make the set-up crystal clear and leave the punch unexplained.
*Example:* Show the blurred "Liked by" name. Unblur it on "mom". No one says "so his mum is the only fan".

**12. Rule of three, then break it.** Beat one sets the pattern, beat two confirms and raises it, beat three breaks it with the biggest hit (NYIT). The third item surprises by going somewhere the pattern did not predict.
*Example:* "Instagram: 5 h. TikTok: 4 h. Netflix: 3 h... content inspiration?"

**13. End on the blow, then stop.** End once the game is pushed as far as it goes (UCB201). The last line is the biggest laugh or a quick button that re-lands it. Python skipped endings by cutting to the next sketch; we can't.
*Example:* Rex: "She's very influential." Cut.

## (b) Writing method: premise to button

1. **Pick the pain (truth).** One real agency frustration from the agency-life map (series bible). Write it as a flat sentence: "Clients judge success by vanity metrics."
2. **Find the comic angle.** Pick one Vorhaus lens: *comic perspective* (Rex sees the world wrongly, consistently), *clash of context* (client behaviour moved elsewhere) or the *wildly inappropriate response*.
3. **State the game in one sentence.** Formula: "Rex [unusual behaviour] because [his logic], and every time Kate [gives him a chance to be sane], he [goes further]." If you need "and also" to say it, you have two games. Cut one.
4. **Set base reality in one frame.** Location, roles and device, carried silently by name tags in second one. Start mid-scene ("the beginning sucks", UCB201); at most 3 lines before the game (Chuffah #3).
5. **Land the first unusual thing by second 3 (short) or second 6 (long).** Kate or the screen frames it so viewers know it is unusual.
6. **List 6 to 10 "if this is true, what else is true" beats**, then keep the best 2 (short) or 3 to 5 (long). Order them by size. Each must be a consequence of the game, not a new game. Chuffah: at most about five game beats, with little space between them.
7. **Insert one explore beat** between heightens in long reels: Rex's reason, said proudly. That makes the next beat feel inevitable.
8. **Write the turn.** The status flip, where Kate's calm move turns Rex's logic back on him. Best turns use what's already on screen: his own words, his own data, his own device (Ep 5 callback). The turn must be unguessable at beat two; test it by stopping after the set-up and guessing.
9. **Write the button.** One short line after the turn, usually Rex trying to save face (the foil stays the foil). Or a silent reaction: Kate's look, a stamp, a status icon. Then cut.
10. **Write the end card as a second beat.** It reuses the sketch's device, lands a wink at the viewer, and gives one reason to care about RecurPost. It must never recap the joke (script-audit S4).
11. **Cut.** Remove every line that neither sets up the game nor heightens it. Remove any line where a character says what the audience just saw. If it is still long, cut a beat, not the pause before the punch.

## (c) Sketch audit (answer yes to every question before building)

1. Can I state the game in one sentence without "and also"?
2. Is the base reality (place, who is who, the device) clear from the first frame, with sound off?
3. Does the first unusual thing land by 3 s (short) or 6 s (long)?
4. Is the pain something most agency owners have personally lived through (would they tag or send it to a colleague)?
5. Is every beat a consequence of the first unusual thing, with no second game?
6. Does each beat get bigger than the last (cost, absurdity or stakes)?
7. Is there a rule-of-three shape, and does the third beat break the pattern rather than repeat it?
8. Is Rex sincere, proud and consistent, never winking?
9. Does Kate do something active (a question, a reveal, a move) rather than just comment?
10. Is the agency side never the butt of the joke, and does Kate win without raising her voice?
11. Is the turn unguessable after beat two?
12. Does the turn use something already established (his words, his data, the device)?
13. Is the slot for the punch visible, but the answer hidden until the punch word?
14. Does the reel end within about 1 s of the blow or button, with nothing after the laugh except the end card?
15. Is there a written pause (0.4 to 1.0 s) before the punch and a short laugh-room hold after it?
16. Does the joke still work muted, with text on screen carrying every essential word?
17. Is there zero dialogue that explains the joke or restates what is on screen?
18. Are names, numbers and objects specific rather than generic?
19. Is the end card a second beat (a wink plus a reason), not a recap, and is RecurPost absent before it?
20. Is the reel under its time cap with the opening at 3 lines or fewer?

## (d) Reference sketches and what to steal

**Monty Python, "Dead Parrot" (Cleese and Chapman, 1969).** Game: a shopkeeper insists an obviously dead parrot is "resting". Heightening: the customer escalates proof (talking to it, shouting at it, banging it on the counter) while the shopkeeper's excuses climb ("stunned", "pining for the fjords"). The biggest reveal: it had been *nailed* to the perch. Then the "ex-parrot" rant. It then drifts into another sketch (Wikipedia; listen2hugh.wordpress.com). **Steal:** the denial game. Rex denies obvious evidence while Kate calmly adds more evidence, and a late fact ("it was nailed there") reframes everything. **Avoid:** the drifting ending.

**Key & Peele, "Substitute Teacher".** Game: Mr. Garvey is sure his wildly wrong pronunciations are right ("Jay-Quellin", "Balakay", "Dee-nice", "A-A-Ron"). Heightening: each correction makes him angrier, up to breaking his clipboard. Button: Timothy survives by copying Garvey's "PRE-sent" (Wikipedia; The Week interview). **Steal:** the foil's certainty plus absurd specifics, and a small list we can predict the shape of but not the next item. The button comes from a side character adapting to the foil's logic.

**Key & Peele, "Continental Breakfast".** Game: a man treats free hotel Froot Loops and mini muffins as fine European cuisine, with growing rapture. Turn: a *Shining* photo reveal suggests he has been at the hotel forever (Slate, 2013). **Steal:** the reveal that recontextualises the whole behaviour in one image. A still frame on the wall screen can do this for us.

**SNL, "More Cowbell" (Will Ferrell, 2000).** Game: a producer demands ever more cowbell on a serious rock recording. Base reality is instant because it borrows a known format (a VH1 *Behind the Music* documentary). Heightening: Gene's playing gets more physical, and the producer keeps asking for more ("I got a fever, and the only prescription is more cowbell"). (Wikipedia; NBC Insider). **Steal:** borrow a familiar format so base reality costs zero seconds, and write one repeatable line that viewers will quote in the comments.

**"The Expert" (Lauris Beinerts, 2014, from Alexey Berezin's story).** Game: a committee, polite and confident, asks the engineer Anderson for seven red lines, all strictly perpendicular, some in green ink, some in transparent ink, one in the shape of a kitten, and treats his "impossible" as unhelpfulness. Heightening: each request is phrased more reasonably than the last while getting less possible, and the project manager "translates" for the client. It ends with Anderson broken ("I can do anything") (IMDb tt3637136; expertdig.com; Laughing Squid). Millions of views, and the top comments are "This is our company." **Steal:** absurd demands in perfectly normal corporate language, and the comment magnet of total recognition. **Flip:** Anderson is a sane man who loses. Kate is a sane woman who wins, so our turn must give her the smarter move (for example, she agrees and delivers exactly what was asked, which exposes the demand).

**Mitchell & Webb, "Are We the Baddies?" (2006).** Game: an SS officer slowly works out from evidence ("our caps have little pictures of skulls on them") that they might be the villains. Heightening: the pieces of evidence pile up, while his partner keeps missing them (Wikipedia; British Comedy Guide). **Steal:** the slow self-discovery through concrete objects. Rex nearly realises he is the problem, gets close, and then misses it. That near-miss is a strong short-form turn and shows the foil's blindness without Kate saying it.

**"The Vendor Client Relationship, in real world situations" (Scofield Editorial).** Game: client behaviour moved into a restaurant, a hair salon and a video store: haggling after the meal, "I'll know it when I see it" haircuts. (CIO; YouTube R2a8TRSgzZY). **Steal:** clash of context. When the in-office version feels tired, move Rex's behaviour to a place where it's plainly absurd, but keep the agency-owner truth in view.

**Cyanide & Happiness (Explosm) shorts.** Irony or a character's reaction is the joke (Wikipedia; TV Tropes). The comics use a silent "beat" panel before the punch, and the shorts use sudden, dark reversals with cheap, readable art. **Steal:** stick figures can be very funny when timing does the work. Use the silent beat, a held reaction face, then one hard cut.

**Corporate Natalie / #WorkTok POV skits.** Almost all are drawn from lived experience or followers' stories, one person plays several labelled roles, and people share because others "feel the same way but maybe are just too afraid to say it" (WorkLife, worklife.news/culture/corporate-work-skits). **Steal:** on-screen role labels, a POV caption hook, and naming the unsayable thing agency owners think about clients.

**Duolingo on TikTok.** The brand wins by entertaining rather than selling, through a consistent mascot character, and ties every "unhinged" moment to a brand truth. (Digiday; HubSpot). **Steal:** the characters, not the logo, are the brand. Keep RecurPost to the end card, but make the end card's wink match the sketch's device so the brand feels part of the joke.

## (e) Short-form addendum

**Platform facts to design for.**
- Most viewers decide in about 2 to 3 s, and 50 to 60% of drop-off happens in the first 3 s (CapCut hooks guide; virvid.ai). Around 80 to 85% of feed video is watched muted (Sprout Social; Instapage), and burned-in captions raise retention by about 15 to 25% (industry estimates).
- On Instagram, shares (especially DM sends) are the strongest ranking signal, above saves (Mosseri, via Hootsuite and Buffer). Design for "I have to send this to my team".
- Replays count as extra watch events. A reel whose last frame flows into its first, or that ends on the image it opened with, gets rewatched (shortzly.com; slidycreator.com).

**Short reel (20 s max).**
- 0 to 1.5 s: base reality done silently (title band, name tags, the device on screen). Rex's first line *is* the first unusual thing. There's no set-up line.
- 1.5 to 11 s: two beats in the pattern, the second bigger. Kate gets one precise line or question.
- 11 to 14 s: written pause, the punch (third beat breaks the pattern), 0.5 to 1 s laugh room, optional face-saving button.
- 14 to 20 s: end card as a second beat.
- About 6 spoken lines in total. One game, one turn, no explore beat. If it needs explaining, it's a long reel.

**Long reel (45 to 80 s).**
- 0 to 5 s: a hook line that orients (who, where, stakes) and makes a promise the ending breaks, said sincerely by Rex. Put it on screen as text too (script-audit check 0).
- 5 to 40 s: three to five heightening beats with one or two explore beats. Change the visual every 3 to 5 s (new chip, screen change, punch-in) so the eye has a reason to stay.
- A mid-point surprise around 55 to 65% of the runtime: a reveal or a new consequence that resets attention. This is where "Continental Breakfast"-style reframes live.
- Final 10 to 15 s: turn, blow, button, end card. No new information after the blow.

**Retention and share mechanics.**
- Text on screen carries every essential word. Captions are word-by-word, speaker-coloured, and at most 5 words per phrase.
- Pauses are the laugh. Hold 0.4 to 1.0 s before the punch; animation lets you freeze Kate's look to the frame.
- Relatability hook: make the pain specific enough that the viewer thinks of *one* client. That's what drives "tag your client" and DM sends.
- Comment bait comes from the content, not from asking: leave one line quotable (the "more cowbell" line), or end on a choice viewers will argue about ("Every agency has one. Which one is yours?" on the end card, used sparingly).
- Loop ending: when possible, the last frame echoes the first (the same screen, the same pose) so the replay feels natural.

**Why viewers get confused or leave, and the fix.**
- *Who is who?* Name tags and role chips ("CLIENT", "AGENCY") in frame one, and consistent character designs.
- *What's the joke?* The first unusual thing arrives late or is buried. Move it to the first line.
- *Two games.* Viewers follow the first and feel the second as noise. Cut it.
- *Explaining.* A character recaps the joke or the end card restates it. Delete it, since it kills the laugh and the loop.
- *Predictable.* The third beat is the obvious one. Break the pattern somewhere true but unexpected.
- *Mean.* Punch up at Rex's behaviour, never at Kate, her team, a junior or the viewer.
