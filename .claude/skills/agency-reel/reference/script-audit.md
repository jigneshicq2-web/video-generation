# Script audit

Run every check before building. Each one comes from a rejection Dinesh caught in a finished draft.

## Short-format checks (run these first; every reel is 20 s max since Ep 9)

**S1. Is it a full joke, and is the turn unguessable?** If the viewer can predict the punch after the second beat (Ep 10 draft 2: once he said "five AM, I'm at the gym" the ending was obvious), it is not punchy. Test: stop after the setup and guess; if the guess is the punch, rewrite. A face-saving button after the punch ("She's very influential.") is a cheap second laugh.

**S1 (original).** Say it as setup, turn, punch. If the last beat is a shrug, an acceptance ("Saved.", "Noted.") or a restatement, it is half a joke (Dinesh, Ep 9 draft 1). The punch must surprise and must follow from the setup: teach a pattern twice, set a bar, break it on the third beat.

**S2. Is the answer's slot on screen, but not the answer?** The field, bubble or line that the punchline fills is visible and empty (or blurred) from the first frame, so the viewer is waiting for it. The screen must NEVER show the answer before the character says it: Ep 10 draft 2 swept a 'customers online' block in on Kate's question and Dinesh: "that kills the joke". Reveal on the punch word (Ep 10: the blurred 'Liked by' name un-blurs on "mom").

**S3. Does it fit?** 5 to 7 spoken lines. Kokoro sketch near 12 to 13 s, whole draft near 18 s, final ElevenLabs cut 20.0 s or less. If it does not fit, cut a line, not the stare before the punch.

**S4. Is the end card a second beat?** It must not re-explain the joke (Ep 9 v1 "Nike taste. Not-Nike budget." was rejected as a recap). It reuses the sketch's device, turns the joke on the agency owner kindly (a wink, never mockery), and gives one verified reason to pick RecurPost, with the claim checked like any other.

**S5. Is this a new area of agency life?** Check the agency-life map in the series bible. A different pain inside the same area as either of the last two reels is not enough.

Long-format checks below still apply, with these changes at 20 s: check 0 shrinks to the first 1 to 2 s and is carried by name tags, the title band and the device (no hook monologue); check 10's 50 to 90 s no longer applies; check 20 becomes one written pause, before the punch; check 21 means about 6 lines.

## 0. Does the first line orient AND hook?

Dinesh, Ep 7 draft 1: "when we start, people don't know what's going on here... the hook is the most important part of the reel." A cold open into the scene loses the scroller.

- In the first 5 to 6 seconds, one line (usually Rex to camera) must say who is who, what the viewer is looking at (the panels, the place, the device), and the stakes.
- It must make a promise the ending pays off or flips ("It took four days. Because everyone had notes."). A hook chip on screen repeats it for muted viewers.
- Present tense, forward-looking. Never narrate the outcome ("It took four days") and then play the story: the viewer thinks it is already over and reads the scene as an unexplained flashback (Dinesh, Ep 7 draft 2). Set up what we are about to watch, ideally as a claim the sketch breaks ("Watch how fast I approve it"), and let it play out in order.
- No sarcasm or bragging in the hook. The foil states his flaw sincerely, as something he is proud of ("Before I approve anything, I run it past my people... That's how you get it right"). "Watch how fast I approve it" read as sarcasm or as him punishing Kate, when their interests are aligned (Dinesh, Ep 7 draft 3). Naming what is coming (the three people) also tells the viewer exactly what the sketch will show.
- Test: show only the first 6 seconds to someone cold. If they can't say who the two people are and what is at stake, rewrite.

## 1. Is this a new sketch, or the last one repainted?

- Different pain from every previous episode (check the pain register, not just the setting).
- Different structure: if the last episode escalated then reversed, this one must not.
- If you can describe this episode and the previous one with the same sentence ("the client messages at night", "the client asks for changes"), it is the same sketch. Start over.

## 2. Does every character behave consistently?

- Kate has RecurPost. She never acts like she lacks a tool she has. If the product solves the problem, she offers it in her first line, and the sketch becomes the client refusing it.
- Kate never participates in the bad path. If she refuses passwords, she does not then ask which dog the password is. Her lines are redirects, dry observations, or the resolution.
- If she waits while things go wrong, show her trying to stop it (interruptions that get talked over), or she reads as passive rather than patient.
- The client can only grant what he owns. He cannot make her a manager on other people's accounts.

## 3. Does the client have a stated motive?

Not "it's faster" when the alternative is obviously faster. Real client motives: "you handle it, don't involve me", "this is how my last agency did it", "I don't trust links", "I want to see it first". The motive has to make his wasted effort his own fault.

Say the motive the way a real person would brush something off, never as a policy statement. "I'm the client. Everything goes through me." was rejected as weird, unnatural and unfunny (Dinesh, Ep 7). "No need. She's right here. Babe?" carries the same motive, sounds real, and sets up the visual joke (she never looks up).

## 4. Are the stakes concrete and early?

"Nothing goes out until it's connected" is policy. "Your sale starts Friday, eleven posts, I can't schedule one of them" is a countdown. State the stakes before the mess starts, and pay them off at the end.

## 5. Does any dead end in the sketch also kill the solution?

The one that has bitten twice:
- If the client fails two-factor, a login-based fix cannot work either.
- If the client is locked out of the account, the viewer assumes he is locked out everywhere, even if technically he is not.

Every obstacle must leave the product's happy path intact, and the script must explain why. Ep 4 resolves this by proving his phone session exists ("You posted a photo from your phone this morning").

## 6. Is the payoff visible before it lands?

The viewer should be able to see the answer sitting there ignored (Ep 4's LINK SENT chip counting up in the corner). New facts introduced only at the end feel abrupt.

## 7. Is risk framed for the person who has to care?

A self-centred client does not care about the agency's other clients. Translate every consequence into his loss: his sale, his account, his money. Keep the agency's side hypothetical if she does not actually work that way ("If I did it your way with every client...").

## 8. Do interruptions actually interrupt?

A dash in the script is not enough. See the build recipe: clip the interrupted line's audio and start the next line with a negative gap so the voices overlap.

## 9. Claims

- Every product claim verified in the repo or the memory files, not from memory.
- Platform claims (Meta restrictions, cascades) confirmed with Dinesh in his words.
- Parity features are stated as what they do, never as unique.
- No fabricated numbers.

## 10. Length and shape

- SHORT FORMAT: 20.0 s max including the end card; see S3. ElevenLabs reads about 15 percent slower than the Kokoro draft.
- LONG FORMAT (back in rotation 2026-09-25): 45 to 75 s final. Aim the Kokoro draft at about 85 percent of the target (a 94-second Kokoro draft landed near 108). S1 to S5 apply to each scene: every scene is a full small joke, and the first laugh lands by about 8 s.
- One idea per reel. If a beat needs a second explanation to work, cut it.
- The last line is the button. It should be short, and it should belong to Kate unless the joke is the client's own words coming back.

## 11. Does the dialogue flow as one conversation?

Dinesh rejected Ep 5 draft 1 as "abrupt, out of nowhere, jump from point to point without explaining or transitioning properly", and draft 2 because the opener did not set up Kate's reply.

Do this pass out loud, line by line, before anything else in this file:
- Each line is a reply to, or a consequence of, the line before it. Test: delete the line. If the next line still makes sense, the two were never connected. Rewrite until nothing can be removed.
- The opener has to invite the reply Kate gives. "I want to talk about last month" does not invite "did you open the report"; "Is it working?" does.
- Every new element (a prop, a screen, a metric, a third person) is asked for or mentioned in a line before it appears on screen. Nothing arrives out of nowhere.
- Stakes, motives and threats are said in dialogue, not carried by stage directions.
- If a line only exists to deliver a fact, cut it or give it a cue from the previous line.

## 12. Are the numbers believable?

"900 clicks is a fiction and no one will agree to it."

- Any figure a character quotes must fit that character's size. Rex is a 400-follower sneaker shop: tens of clicks, not hundreds.
- If a number cannot be sized credibly, make it relative ("more than the rest of the month") instead of absolute.

## 13. Is it evergreen?

- No month names, dates, weekdays with dates, or "this Friday" in dialogue or on screen mocks. The reel is watched whenever it surfaces. Say "last month", "next month", "four months", "Monday" only as a recurring weekday.
- Screen mocks show weekday and subject, never a date.

## 14. Does Kate respect the client?

- Kate never handles the client's private data: no scrolling his inbox, no taking his phone, no reading his messages. If his own screen is the evidence, he opens it himself and she only asks.
- She never says anything about him that she could only know by snooping.

## 15. Does a visual land on the wrong word?

Dinesh, Ep 8 draft 1: three customers popped into the window on "hire young people, hand them the keys", so they read as the people Rex hired, and "A line" did not say whose line.
- Never cue a new person or object on a word that could name it wrongly. Bring the queue in on a neutral beat (or an ironic one: the first customer arrives on "I haven't even read it").
- When something new appears, the line that points at it says what it is ("Customers. Lining up outside."), not just "that".

## 16. Does every character choice have a stated reason? (Ep 8)

Dinesh caught three in one episode:
- "He declined two calls, why does he pick up the third?" Every change in behaviour needs a line that motivates it, in character ("Let's put her on the video": the call now serves his recording).
- "No owner would ignore a customer asking if it's 70% off." The foil engages with everything in front of him; his flaw shows in HOW he engages (brushes it off: "Nice try, buddy"), never in pretending it isn't there.
- A callback with no setup ("Checking slows a team down" after its setup line was cut for length). When trimming, re-run check 11 on every payoff line.

## 17. Does the foil crack gradually? (Ep 8)

Rex went from "leadership" to "What did Tyler post?" in one line and Dinesh rejected it as too sudden. The foil's arc is denial, spin, crack, face-saving. Let the other character deliver the bad news in pieces ("Seventy percent off." ... "On everything.") so he has room to spin in between, and let him save face after the crack ("New policy." / "Great leaders listen.") instead of accepting the mistake.

## 18. Is every text-only payoff also said out loud?

Anything the joke depends on (Tyler's apology) must be spoken, not only shown on a small screen mock. Viewers do not read a banner in the second it is up.

## 19. Is the hook chip about the opening, not the twist?

"He hasn't read his intern's sale post" was rejected: the opening is about his leadership video, so a chip about the twist is irrelevant there. If the device (REC screen, "Leadership tips" label, his own first line) already orients the viewer, drop the chip.

## 20. Are the pauses written in? (Ep 8)

Dinesh, Ep 8 draft 6: "there was space for pauses to let viewers process what was happening. Some pauses add a nice touch to comedy." Mark every [beat] in the script, and give it 1 to 2.5 s in GAP: the realisation (he reads it, turns to the crowd), the reaction to a dry line ("I called you twice." ... "Okay. Okay."), the decision (stares at the approval, then a tiny "Approved."), and the held grin before the button. A wall of back-to-back lines kills the laugh.

## 21. Fewer beats, each one earned (Ep 8)

Ep 8 draft 6's second half was rejected as "all over the place": spin, crack, callback, face-saving line, a read-out, approval and button all stacked. The fix was three moves (discovery, panic call, the fix that needs the thing he refused) where every earlier boast pays off once, in order. If the second half has more than about 10 lines, cut to the moves.

## 22. Does a prop reveal too early? (Ep 8)

Dinesh: the customer asked "is it 70% off?" while already holding the post up, so Rex reading it later made no sense. A piece of evidence stays hidden until the beat that needs it (phone down while asking, slapped on the glass only after "Nice try, buddy"). Same for crowds: nobody appears in the background until a line or a look introduces them, or they read as random extras.

## 23. Does the in-world audience already know it?

If the character is talking to an in-world audience (his followers), he does not tell them what they already know ("Rex here, I own a sneaker shop"). Carry that for the viewer with a name tag and the set.

## 24. Does the brand name land right after a name-shaped slot? (Dinesh 2026-09-27, Pick Your Brain)

"You already know his name." followed by "RecurPost. For the clients who sign." made it sound like his name was RecurPost. Never put the spoken brand name straight after a line that asks for or points at a name, a person or an answer: the viewer hears the brand as the answer. Either leave the brand out of the voiceover (the stamp and URL carry it; "We can't help with him. We can help with the clients who sign." shipped) or put a full thought between them.

## 25. Does anyone ask a question the screen already answered? (Dinesh 2026-09-27, Real People)

Kate asked "Names?" after the watermark was already on screen, and Dinesh: "that doesn't sound real... why would she say names? We know it's a stock photo." Once the device shows the answer, a question about it sounds staged. Kate states what she sees, calmly ("That's a stock photo."), and the foil's punch rebuts her statement ("They're real people."), which is stronger because it answers her directly and calls back to his first line.
